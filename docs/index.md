# CEDAR

## Curricular and Enrollment Data Analytics and Reporting 

**As a dashboard**, CEDAR goes deeper than headcounts and section lists. It surfaces what's actually happening across curriculum and enrollment: which courses are trending toward low enrollment before the drop deadline, how DFW rates vary by section, where students are hitting walls in required sequences, how declared major patterns are moving term by term. The analyses are built around the questions that keep coming up in meetings. CEDAR provide specific, time-sensitive intelligence that shapes administrative processes, curriculum, and advising. A question answered once can be answered again automatically, for the next term, by the next administrator in the role.

**As a platform**, CEDAR is how analytical work can stop evaporating. There are far too many well intentioned spreadsheets and plots that convey useful data at a moment in time, but are never reproducible when the follow-up comes two years later. When a question is worth answering, the way to the answer itself is worth preserving. CEDAR keeps analysis in open source code that's modular, extensible, and well documented. When different data teams need to provide data, they have a common platform to work from. The institutional knowledge that typically lives in an analyst's inbox or a spreadsheet becomes public infrastructure instead.

## Values

**Transparency**

Every CEDAR analysis is produced by source code in a public repository. The methodology isn't a separate document — it's the calculation itself. When there's a question about how something was counted, we can show exactly how that number was derived, what was included, and what was not. It's commented in the code, it's echoed in every tab in the app. That's standard in research. Why not for institutional analytics, too?


**Trust**

There are a lot of ways to count enrollment, fail rates, and everything else. Census date or current? Crosslisted sections deduplicated or separate? Undergraduates only, or all levels? When a department chair and a dean's office produce different numbers from "the same" data, it's usually not an error — it's different choices, often made without realizing a choice was involved. A shared platform means shared definitions, or ar least clearly defined differences. When numbers diverge, the question becomes answerable: here's the code, here's what's different.

**Access**

CEDAR exists to answer the questions that chairs and associate deans actually ask: which sections are trending toward low enrollment before the drop deadline, how does this term's composition compare to three years ago, where are students hitting walls in a required sequence. The actual questions, answerable when the question is fresh.
